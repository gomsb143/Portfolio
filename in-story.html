What I Learned

Getting it wrong. Then changing.

I made myself the single point of coordination. When I stepped away, the team struggled — and that was on me.

One of the biggest misconceptions about programme management is that a PM simply attends meetings and schedules calls. Much of the work — managing stakeholder expectations, resolving conflicts, negotiating priorities, removing roadblocks — happens behind the scenes, often invisible to the team.

I experienced this firsthand during maternity leave. A temporary Project Manager stepped in while I remained available in the background. For the first time, the technical leads were directly exposed to stakeholder conversations I had previously managed on their behalf — difficult discussions around changing priorities, urgent escalations, conflicting expectations, and release commitments.

That release struggled. Looking back, the issue wasn't the temporary PM. It was my approach. In trying to protect the engineering team from distractions, I had unintentionally made myself the single point of coordination. Too much stakeholder knowledge, context, and decision-making sat with me alone.

That experience fundamentally changed how I lead. Today, technical leads regularly participate in stakeholder discussions, understand business context, and share ownership of key decisions. Clear documentation, backup ownership, no project depends on a single individual. The team is stronger for it.
